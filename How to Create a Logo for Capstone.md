Before you get started
Your project needs a logo for your website, GitHub README, case study, linkedin, etc. You don’t need design experience, but you should end up with what a designer would hand a client: a small, consistent set of files and a simple style guide. Timebox this to a half day, a day at most. It shouldn’t compete with your featureset work.

Try to approach the design process with an open mind and have fun with this when you can. Please note there are some limitations to the logo process where compromises may be necessary.

Before creating your logo try to think about / answer the following questions. If you can come up with a single sentence or paragraph for the below, this will be extremely useful for the logo process later on.

What is the project’s name and why was it given this name?
What does your project do? Who is your project designed for?
What are some logos/Capstone logos that could be used as a reference for your logo?
What do your current market competitors logos look like?
What sort of feeling would you like your logo to exude? What symbols, shapes and/or colors do you want to incorporate into your logo?

How do I make a logo?
Claude can take you from idea to finished logo package. It writes logos as SVG code and renders them so you can see them, which means everything it makes is editable and can live in your repo. It can then export the PNGs, favicon, and other files you need from that SVG.

Tips for using AI

Start a dedicated conversation and paste your brand brief (step 1 below) at the top, so every response is grounded in it.
Ask for range, not one answer. “Give me 6 concepts in different directions” beats “make me a logo.” Ask Claude to put them side by side on one page so you can compare.
Ask it to explain its choices. Why this shape, this color, this spacing? You’ll pick up design vocabulary fast, and you’ll get better at giving feedback.
Give specific feedback. “Make it better” goes nowhere. “Thicker strokes, drop the second color, more space between the icon and the name” works.
Ask for critique. Have Claude evaluate its concepts against your brief and the tests in step 6. Ask what a professional designer would criticize.
Iterate in small steps. Change one or two things at a time so you know what’s helping.
You make the call. AI will happily produce variations forever. Decide as a team, and stop when it passes the tests.

Steps

1. Define the brand first

Write a few sentences on what your project is, who it’s for, and three adjectives for how it should feel (e.g., “fast, minimal, trustworthy”). Include this in every prompt. Without it, you’ll get generic results.

2. Collect references

Find 5–10 logos from developer tools you think look good. Note what they have in common. Usually it’s simple shapes, few colors, and generous spacing. These are your quality bar, not something to copy.

3. Give the AI a direction

Vague prompts get generic logos. Give Claude something concrete to work from:

An aesthetic or era: “80s retro-futuristic,” “Swiss minimalism,” “brutalist,” “early-internet terminal,” “Japanese railway signage.”
A metaphor for what your project does: a tool that routes traffic might suggest a switchboard, a river delta, or a conductor. Brainstorm a few metaphors with Claude before designing anything, then pick the one that fits your story.
Visual inspiration: paste in a movie poster, album cover, product, or photo whose mood you like, and ask Claude what makes it work (palette, type, composition) before applying those qualities to your logo.

Borrow the feel but don't violate copyright. Never ask for something that copies a specific existing logo or artwork.

Expect a strong style to show up more in your colors, fonts, and site design than in the logo itself, which still needs to pass the tests in step 6. A good result is a simple mark that feels like it belongs to the larger aesthetic.

4. Generate concepts as code

Ask Claude to design logo concepts directly as SVG. Get several directions: abstract marks, lettermarks, symbols tied to your metaphor. Render them, pick 2–3 as a team, and iterate on those in code: adjust shapes, spacing, and colors, and keep versions in your repo. You can use an image generator for early inspiration, but your final logo should be clean SVG you can read and edit.

5. Use a real typeface for the name

Pick a font for your wordmark (Google Fonts is a good source) rather than having AI draw the letters. Use the same font across your site and case study.

6. Test it, don’t just eyeball it

Your logo should pass all of these:

Recognizable at 16×16 in a browser tab
Works in solid black and solid white
Recognizable when blurred or shrunk to a thumbnail
Holds up on both light and dark backgrounds

If it fails, simplify. Fewer shapes and fewer colors almost always fix it.

7. Check originality

Make sure it doesn’t resemble an existing product’s logo, especially your model project’s or other dev tools’. Do a quick image search. AI can reproduce familiar designs without telling you.

8. Get an outside opinion

Before finalizing, show your top choice to someone with a design eye: a mentor, a teammate with design background, or a friend.

9. Deliver the package

Your final logo folder should include:

Primary logo (icon + name), plus the icon alone and the wordmark alone
Each in full color, black, and white versions
SVG and transparent PNG for each
Favicon (SVG plus a 32×32 PNG) and a 512×512 PNG app/avatar icon
A social preview image (1200×630) for link sharing and your GitHub repo
Design tokens: a small CSS file with your colors and fonts as variables, plus a short README noting how much clear space to leave around the logo

And one final thing about logos: this branding process should be lighthearted and fun. Try not to get too caught up with trying to create a super serious logo.

General tips and hints on how to pick a great looking logo

Refine your search with Fiverr's keyword algorithm
Fiverr designs use a tagging system where designers add tags to their designs to bring up relevant searches for the user. To refine your search and get more targeted designs follow the tips below:

experiment with keywords, You can always edit your search parameters to bring up new designs
use a mind map to generate related visual keywords
think about the symbols that your project represents. If your project incorporates an eagle, pick related words such as "beak", "wings", "bird" etc.
Include the first letter of your chosen project name
Include the word "abstract" as many software logos use abstract geometric forms. Shape words also work well here too ("hexagon", "triangle" etc.)

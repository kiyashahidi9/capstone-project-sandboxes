Thus far, we've explored engineering domains, tools, and services with a focus on the problems that drove their development.

Starting today, we're going to switch gears to look at how you can use your research to come up with your own Capstone project ideas.

The core of a Capstone project is a clear use case. A project's use case answers a few critical questions:

What are the core engineering challenges that drive this project?
Who experiences this problem? What are the characteristics of the application or development team? What are their requirements?
What set of tradeoffs define who this project is not for?
Where does this project sit in relation to alternative solutions? For whom (or for what types of applications) would it be an optimal approach?
Analogy: what is the use case of a toothbrush?
Imagine your team has decided to build a toothbrush. In order to move forward with your project proposal, your team's mentor asks that you clarify the use case of a toothbrush:

What is the problem that a toothbrush solves?
Teeth accumulate plaque and other substances that cause them to decay
Who experiences this particular challenge
humans with teeth
not for people with no teeth (babies) or non-humans with teeth (cats)
Where does this project sit in relation to alternative solutions? What set of tradeoffs define who this is not for?
Legacy option: salt and water (people used this before toothbrushes; less effective but easy)
Enterprise option: Daily dental hygienist visits (very expensive, more time-consuming, but better coverage)
DIY option: build your own toothbrush out of twigs (requires more expertise)
Flossing: Another option, but has a slightly different use case
By mapping out the cases when a toothbrush is appropriate, as well as the tradeoffs of alternative options, we can see that a toothbrush will be less comprehensive than the enterprise option, but also more accessible and easier to use.

Finding a use case for your project
Defining the use case for your project will require understanding the use case for existing alternative solutions in that problem space.

This will require the type of research that we've been practicing over the past week, which is why that was our starting point.

We will walk through the process of using a top-down research approach to narrow down to a specific use case in another document, but broadly, the process will involve:

Identifying the problem space that you are interested in
Identifying the various existing solutions
Mapping out the use cases of those solutions
Identifying an alternative set of tradeoffs that your project might take to satisfy a different use case (adjacent problem, narrower feature set, etc).
Validating the use case of your project
Sometimes a team will develop a project proposal that sounds like a reasonable idea and may even make a different set of tradeoffs than an existing project. However, staff or mentors will push back by asking "is it a valid use case?"

By this, what we want to understand is whether the use case you've defined really exists -- are there developers who actually experience the problem you describe and want the set of tradeoffs you are proposing?

Evidence of a use case can take many forms, from articles to forum discussions to existing tools. Ideally, you want to be able to describe an engineering problem and have other professional engineers say, "YES, I've absolutely experienced that, and it is a pain to resolve."

As with our bottom-up research, the key to effective research is to not rely solely on the marketing materials on the project or service's website. Those materials will of course present the best possible case for their project, which usually means focusing on the features that expand the project's use case ("This is the all-in-one solution for your XYZ needs!"). Marketing material rarely focuses on who the project is not for.

Instead, look for articles, forum posts, social media threads, etc. of people that are using a given project or working in the problem space you are researching. Their experiences will help to illustrate the use cases, as well as the limitations and tradeoffs of existing solutions.

Tip: Don't treat project research as purely consumptive; many teams have had success by reaching out to forum posters, blog authors, and library maintainers to ask them more directed questions about the use case and limitations of a given project. It's helpful to clarify that you are working on a student project and want to understand something a bit better. Many folks are happy to help!
